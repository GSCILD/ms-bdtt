# 2. Related work

## 2.1 Cross-domain fault diagnosis under variable operating conditions

Vibration patterns of bearings and gears depend strongly on rotational speed and load, a phenomenon well documented in the rotating-machinery diagnostic literature [13]. The fault characteristic frequencies of rolling-element bearings, namely the ball-pass frequency on the inner race (BPFI), the ball-pass frequency on the outer race (BPFO), the ball-spin frequency (BSF) and the fundamental train frequency (FTF), scale linearly with shaft rotational frequency. When speed varies, these frequencies shift proportionally, so feature descriptors computed in a fixed frequency band become unreliable. Order tracking, which resamples vibration signals at constant angular increments rather than at constant time intervals, normalises the spectral content across speeds; however, it requires precise shaft-angle measurements that are not always available on board, and it does not address load-dependent amplitude modulation.

Recent reviews systematically classify deep-learning approaches for rotating-machinery diagnosis under variable operating conditions and identify speed-invariant feature learning and domain adaptation as the two principal strategies [3, 13]. Load variations affect fault signatures through several mechanisms: higher loads increase contact stresses in bearings and gear teeth, amplifying the impulse amplitudes produced by localised defects while simultaneously raising the background noise floor through increased structural excitation. The signal-to-noise ratio therefore varies non-monotonically with load. Very low loads produce weak fault impulses buried in sensor noise, whereas very high loads generate strong structural vibrations that mask fault-related transients, so intermediate load ranges usually offer the best detectability. This non-monotonicity complicates the design of load-invariant feature extractors. In marine diesel engines, load variations are further compounded by sea-state-induced speed fluctuations, because propeller slip superimposes a quasi-sinusoidal speed modulation on the mean operating speed [14].

Marine-specific diagnosis introduces additional challenges beyond variable operating conditions. Compared with terrestrial installations, marine machinery exhibits distinct fault signatures because seawater-induced corrosion, biofouling and wave-induced hull vibration act simultaneously; chloride-induced corrosion in particular accelerates bearing degradation several-fold and fundamentally alters fault progression. Characterisations of the marine vibration environment further show that hull-borne noise from wave impact and propeller cavitation creates a broadband low-frequency background below 500 Hz that overlaps machinery-related fault frequencies, which makes feature extraction particularly difficult in the marine context [14, 15]. These findings indicate that methods validated on clean laboratory data may degrade severely when deployed at sea.

A growing body of work learns speed- and load-invariant representations directly from raw vibration signals [6, 5, 13]. Time-frequency convolutional networks operating on multi-resolution spectrograms generalise better across speeds than time-domain convolutional networks; multi-scale attention modules that fuse features at different temporal resolutions improve robustness to speed variation; and frequency-domain adaptive networks dynamically adjust convolutional kernel sizes according to operating speed, removing the need for explicit order tracking. However, these methods still assume that training and test data share the same marginal distribution of operating conditions, an assumption that is violated in marine applications where vessels operate over fundamentally different speed–load profiles.

The transfer-learning community has developed several strategies for handling distributional shift. Transfer component analysis projects source and target data into a common subspace in which their first-order moments match [16]; deep CORAL aligns second-order statistics [17]; MMD-based methods measure distributional divergence in an RKHS [18]; and domain-adversarial neural networks employ a gradient-reversal layer that discourages a domain discriminator from distinguishing source from target [19]. Whereas these methods are effective for discrete domain pairs, they do not naturally extend to continuous operating-condition manifolds, where the source–target relationship is gradational rather than binary. For mechanical systems specifically, physics-informed features have been reported to outperform purely data-driven features under large domain gaps [20], which suggests that mechanistic priors should play a central role in cross-domain fault diagnosis.

## 2.2 Domain adaptation methods for mechanical systems

Domain adaptation methods for mechanical systems fall into four broad categories. Statistical moment-matching methods, including MMD, CORAL and joint distribution adaptation, minimise the discrepancy between source and target features by aligning their first- and second-order statistics [18, 17]. These methods are computationally efficient but are limited to linear or near-linear distribution shifts. Adversarial adaptation methods use generative adversarial networks or gradient reversal to learn domain-invariant features [19]; conditional adversarial domain adaptation additionally introduces entropy minimisation for more discriminative features [21]. Although adversarial methods capture non-linear shifts, they suffer from training instability and mode collapse, particularly when the source and target distributions overlap only partially. Prototype-based methods learn class-specific centroid representations in a shared feature space and enforce class-conditional distribution alignment, which works well when fault classes are well separated but struggles with the overlapping distributions typical of noisy industrial environments [12, 22]. Metric-learning approaches learn distance functions that bring same-class samples closer and push different-class samples apart across domains, jointly optimising intra-class consistency and inter-domain alignment [23].

Despite their success on benchmark datasets, existing domain adaptation methods face three limitations in marine power applications. First, they treat domains as fixed entities rather than as points on a continuous manifold and therefore fail to exploit the smooth transitions between operating conditions. Second, they assume access to labelled source data and to either labelled or unlabelled target data, but not the intermediate situation in which neither the source nor the target condition of interest is fully labelled. Third, they aggregate information from all available source domains equally, ignoring the fact that some source conditions are more relevant to the target than others. Recent physics-guided source-free schemes have begun to address relevance and label scarcity [24, 25], but they still operate on discrete domain pairs. The framework proposed here addresses these limitations through continuous domain interpolation, unlabelled-target adaptation and attention-weighted sample selection.

## 2.3 Mechanistic simulation and digital twin frameworks

Mechanistic simulation for rotating machinery covers lumped-parameter models, finite element models and multi-body dynamics. Lumped-parameter thermal models capture temperature distributions across cylinder heads and turbochargers for engine health monitoring [10], while multi-body dynamics models describe gearbox vibration under variable load, incorporating tooth-flank contact mechanics and bearing flexibility [26, 27]. These models provide physically interpretable fault signatures, but their computational cost limits real-time application and their accuracy depends critically on precise parameter specification. For marine propulsion systems in particular, rotordynamic simulation has been established as a key tool for predicting shaft-line vibration characteristics; established formulations account for bearing nonlinearity and misalignment effects [26], and recent work identifies nonlinear dynamic parameters of thrust bearings from experimental data [28].

Digital twin frameworks for rotating machinery have attracted considerable attention. A five-dimensional digital-twin model comprising physical, virtual, data, service and network dimensions was proposed in [7]; digital-twin-enabled predictive maintenance architectures emphasise bidirectional data flow between physical and virtual spaces [1]; and digital-twin applications for rotating-machinery diagnosis have been categorised by their level of physics integration, from purely data-driven twins to fully physics-constrained models [2]. Frameworks specific to ship propulsion systems integrate real-time sensor data with physics-based models for condition monitoring and report improved fault-detection rates over purely data-driven approaches [10, 29], which supports the value of physics integration in marine applications.

Physics-informed neural networks represent a hybrid approach that embeds governing equations as regularisation terms during training [30]. In the fault-diagnosis context, they enforce physical consistency in generated synthetic data and reduce the simulator-to-measurement gap compared with purely data-driven generators [20]. However, they require differentiable physical models and careful tuning of loss weights, which limits their applicability to complex mechanical systems with discontinuous dynamics.

The digital-twin literature has largely focused on single-equipment monitoring rather than on cross-domain transfer. Surveys of digital-twin applications for ship operations indicate that most existing work addresses predictive maintenance for individual components rather than cross-domain knowledge transfer [2, 15], and data scarcity remains a fundamental bottleneck for intelligent condition monitoring on board vessels. Existing digital-twin frameworks generally assume that the virtual model is calibrated for one specific physical asset and pay limited attention to transferring knowledge across assets or operating conditions. The present work extends the digital-twin paradigm by introducing a continuous bridge distribution that interpolates between laboratory and field conditions, enabling knowledge transfer without asset-specific recalibration for every new deployment.

## 2.4 Summary and positioning

Current methods handle discrete domain pairs without continuous interpolation, lack demand-guided sampling strategies, rarely combine mechanistic simulation with uncertainty quantification, and ignore sample relevance weighting in multi-source aggregation. Table 1 summarises the positioning of MS-BDTT relative to existing approaches.

Table 1. Comparison of cross-domain fault diagnosis methods in terms of five capability dimensions.

| Method category | Continuous domain | Demand-guided sampling | Mechanistic simulation | Attention weighting | Unlabelled target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Statistical DA (MMD, CORAL) | No | No | No | No | Partial |
| Adversarial DA (DANN, CDAN) | No | No | No | No | Yes |
| Physics-informed / PINN methods | No | No | Yes | No | No |
| Prototype and metric learning | No | No | No | No | Partial |
| Digital twin with DA | No | No | Yes | No | Partial |
| Physics-guided source-free DA | No | No | Partial | Partial | Yes |
| MS-BDTT (this work) | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |

To the best of our knowledge, MS-BDTT is the first framework to address all five capability dimensions simultaneously. The continuous domain bridge enables smooth interpolation across the operating envelope; demand-guided sampling optimises the allocation of computational resources; mechanistic simulation provides physically grounded synthetic data; Bayesian calibration bridges the simulator-to-measurement gap with uncertainty quantification; and attention weighting counteracts negative transfer by selectively exploiting relevant bridge samples. Together, these components form a coherent response to the marine power fault diagnosis challenge.

# 3. Methodology

Figure 1 gives a conceptual overview of the framework. MS-BDTT comprises three stages. Stage 1 generates synthetic fault signatures with a parameterised rotordynamic simulator over a continuous operating envelope and allocates the simulation budget according to a demand map (Sections 3.1–3.3). Stage 2 calibrates the simulator against unlabelled reference measurements with a Bayesian particle filter (Section 3.4). Stage 3 performs attention-weighted adversarial transfer from the calibrated continuous bridge to the unlabelled target domain (Sections 3.5 and 3.6). ## 3.1 Parameterised rotordynamic simulation

The parameterised rotordynamic simulator generates synthetic fault signatures across a continuous operating envelope. It is built on a two-node Timoshenko beam element formulation that captures shaft bending, torsion and axial dynamics simultaneously (Appendix A). Each element accounts for shear deformation and rotary inertia, which is required for accurate prediction of the higher-order modes that become diagnostically relevant at elevated speeds. The global equation of motion is

M q̈ + (C + Ω G) q̇ + K q = F(t)    (1)

where M, C, G and K denote the mass, structural damping, gyroscopic and stiffness matrices, Ω is the rotational speed, q is the generalised displacement vector and F(t) collects external excitations, including unbalance forces, gear-mesh harmonics and bearing contact forces. Faults are inserted through localised stiffness reduction in the bearing support elements: a partial crack in the outer race of a deep-groove ball bearing appears as a periodic variation of the radial stiffness at the ball-pass frequency, while shaft misalignment introduces an asymmetric bending stiffness that generates a 2× running-speed component [26, 28]. Appendix B summarises the finite element assembly and the parameter values used in this study.

In contrast to discrete domain adaptation, a continuous domain bridge B interpolates between the source model M_S and the target model M_t over the full operating envelope O_op:

B = ⋃ₖ { x̃_sim(θ_o(k) + Δθ) : Δθ ∈ ΔΘ_bridge, k = 1, …, K }    (2)

where θ_o collects the operating parameters (speed, mean effective pressure and shaft alignment), K is the number of interpolation nodes and ΔΘ_bridge is the interpolation neighbourhood. Because the simulator is differentiable with respect to θ_o, bridge samples can be generated at any point of the envelope rather than only at preset working points, which is precisely the property exploited in Stage 2 and Stage 3.

## 3.2 Noise injection and sensor model

Structured noise that mimics marine sensor characteristics is injected into the raw simulated vibration:

x_noisy = x_sim + n_sensor(·) + n_ambient(·)    (3)

The sensor-noise term n_sensor models accelerometer-specific artefacts, including frequency-dependent noise floors (white noise below 1 kHz and 1/f noise above), quantisation error of 24-bit analogue-to-digital converters and baseline drift caused by temperature-induced sensor offsets. The ambient-noise term n_ambient models marine environmental disturbances, namely hull vibration transmitted through engine mounting compliance, wave-induced excitation between 0.1 and 2 Hz, and flow turbulence around the propeller blades. All noise parameters are calibrated to match the spectral characteristics of marine-grade accelerometers (for example the PCB 352C33 class) under sea-trial conditions reported in the literature [14].

The sensor model further includes a frequency response function H_s(f) that captures the resonant behaviour of the accelerometer mounting system. Above the mounting resonance (approximately 8 kHz for stud-mounted configurations) the response rolls off at about −12 dB per octave, attenuating the high-frequency content that would otherwise be unrealistically clean in simulation. This keeps the spectral characteristics of the synthetic data realistic over the whole measurement bandwidth.

## 3.3 Demand-guided sample allocation

The demand map m(θ_o) quantifies the diagnostic difficulty at each operating point. It combines an MMD-based measure of domain separation with the fault-sensitivity gradient:

m(θ_o) = α · d_MMD( M_S(θ_o), M_t ) + (1 − α) · |∂κ/∂s|    (4)

where d_MMD is the maximum mean discrepancy between the source and target distributions at the operating point θ_o, |∂κ/∂s| is the fault-sensitivity gradient, that is, the rate of change of the classification accuracy κ with respect to fault severity s, and α = 0.6 weights the two terms. The weighting prioritises domain separation, in agreement with the observation that domain-level discrepancy dominates class-level shift in cross-domain fault diagnosis [3]. The value α = 0.6 was selected by grid search over {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8} on the S1 validation split (Appendix D). To respect the partition restrictions of Section 4.4, the MMD entering the demand map is computed exclusively on source-training and simulator samples; no target-domain data, labelled or unlabelled, enter the demand map or the bridge construction. The MMD term is estimated with the V-statistic estimator of Eq. (12) using a Gaussian RBF kernel whose length-scale is set by the median heuristic, that is, the median pairwise distance between pooled source-training and simulated features; the sensitivity term is approximated by training a reference classifier at each operating point with varying fault severities and computing the finite difference of the resulting accuracy.

Given a total budget of N_tot bridge samples, the allocation to interpolation node k is proportional to its demand score:

N_k = N_tot · m(θ_o(k)) / Σⱼ m(θ_o(j))    (5)

so that the total computational budget is preserved while samples are concentrated where they matter most. In practice the allocation is clipped to a minimum of five samples per node to avoid starving any region of the envelope.

All quantitative statements about sampling efficiency in this paper rest on measured sweeps. The budget sweep covers both allocation rules at six budget levels (20, 40, 60, 80, 100 and 120 samples per condition) with five independent runs per point (Appendix D.2, Table D.3; Fig. 10). On this basis the demand-guided rule reaches 90 % of its own full-budget accuracy with 60 samples per condition against 100 for the uniform grid, a measured budget-efficiency gain of 1.67×, and reaches the same 75.5 % five-scenario accuracy with 60 against 80 samples (1.33×). The uniform grid attains a higher full-budget accuracy (86.8 % versus 83.9 %), so the demand-guided rule is recommended for its steeper early budget curve rather than for a higher ceiling. An earlier version of this manuscript quoted a fit-derived uniform-grid threshold of approximately 102 samples and a 1.7× gain; the measured sweep supersedes both figures.
