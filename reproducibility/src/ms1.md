# Abstract

Marine power systems face a fundamental data limitation: labelled fault examples remain scarce across the global fleet, whereas operating conditions drift continuously across vessels, sea states and service ages. This paper proposes MS-BDTT (Marine-power-System Bayesian Digital Twin Transfer), a three-stage framework that connects controlled laboratory measurements to unlabelled synthetic target domains through mechanistic simulation and physics-informed domain transfer. Throughout this study the unlabelled synthetic target domain is represented by a synthetic ship propulsion fault dataset (SPFD); all marine claims are therefore simulation-validated rather than vessel-validated. Stage one builds a parameterised rotordynamic simulator that produces synthetic fault signatures over a continuous operating envelope spanned by rotational speed, mean effective pressure and shaft alignment. A demand-guided sampler allocates the computational budget in proportion to a composite diagnostic-difficulty map; in a measured budget sweep (five runs per point) it reaches 90 % of its own full-budget accuracy with 60 samples per condition against 100 for uniform grid sampling, a measured budget-efficiency gain of 1.67×, and it attains the same 75.5 % accuracy with 60 against 80 samples (1.33×). Stage two calibrates the simulator against sparse, unlabelled reference sensor readings with a Bayesian particle filter, reducing the simulator-to-simulator discrepancy by 65.7 % in maximum mean discrepancy (MMD). Stage three introduces an attention-weighted adversarial network that suppresses the 37 % of bridge samples with the lowest learned relevance to mitigate negative transfer; the measured retention sweep peaks at that 63 % operating point (83.9 % five-scenario macro-average against 81.2 % without suppression and 77.4 % at 20 % retention). Experiments on four public rotating-machinery datasets, a high-fidelity marine propulsion simulator (MarineSim, validated against published performance data of a MAN 48/60CR-class medium-speed engine) and the synthetic SPFD generated by MarineSim show that MS-BDTT attains a five-scenario macro-average accuracy of 83.9 % (range 76.0–96.5 %) and 76.0 ± 3.5 % in the fully unlabelled-target scenario, exceeding the strongest baseline by 3.6 and 6.8 percentage points, respectively. A ten-seed paired replication confirms these margins (+3.58 ± 0.58 pp, 95 % CI [3.16, 4.00] on the macro-average; +6.83 ± 1.25 pp, 95 % CI [5.94, 7.72] on S5), all results are regenerated from a single auditable source, and the complete reproducibility package — code, split manifests, seeds, baseline configurations, weights and run-level predictions — is deposited on Zenodo (Code and Data Availability). Because the three training runs of the full evaluation grid are insufficient for confirmatory inference, all grid-level comparisons are labelled exploratory, and conclusions are bounded accordingly.

**Keywords:** Continuous domain interpolation; Continuous domain adaptation; Digital twin; Cross-domain fault diagnosis; Bayesian calibration; Marine power systems; Rotordynamic simulation; Adversarial transfer learning; Ship propulsion; Edge deployment

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background and motivation

Marine power systems, comprising propulsion diesel engines, gas turbines, reduction gearboxes, shaft bearings and auxiliary generators, form the operational backbone of any vessel. These components endure decades of service across conditions ranging from calm harbour waters to beam seas with wave heights exceeding 15 m. Seawater accelerates material degradation three to five times faster than terrestrial exposure because of chloride-induced corrosion and biofouling. Propeller loading cycles impose low-cycle fatigue on shafting systems with stress ranges of 200–400 MPa, well within the endurance limit of modern alloy steels but subject to scatter from manufacturing defects and surface finish variations. Thermal gradients across engine blocks exceeding 80 °C generate stress concentrations at critical interfaces such as cylinder-liner bore surfaces and crankcase joints. Hull vibration induced by wave impact adds broadband structural loading that complicates fault-feature extraction from accelerometer signals, particularly below 500 Hz, where machinery-related vibrations overlap with hull-mode responses.

The economic stakes are substantial: propulsion-related failures generate costs estimated in the literature at billions of US dollars annually worldwide, including direct repair costs, vessel downtime, cargo-delay penalties and environmental remediation. Beyond economics, safety considerations drive the regulatory agenda. The IMO digitalisation strategy (MSC.1/Circ.1598) mandates standardised ship–shore data exchange protocols, while autonomous vessel programmes progressively remove the human inspection layer that traditionally detected incipient faults during routine deck rounds. Classification societies, including DNV, Lloyd's Register and ClassNK, increasingly require data-driven evidence for smart-ship certification, pushing owners towards condition-based maintenance rather than time-based replacement schedules [1, 2]. This study reports no energy-efficiency or fuel-consumption measurements; sustainability considerations motivate the work but are not quantified anywhere in this paper.

It is worth emphasising that labelled fault samples from operational vessels are extremely scarce; most shipboard sensor recordings contain normal-condition measurements only, without ground-truth fault annotations. This practical constraint makes the unlabelled-target diagnostic scenario (scenario S5 in Section 4.2) the most meaningful setting for marine fault diagnosis, and it is the setting on which the present study places its main emphasis.

The gap between laboratory performance and deployed-vessel reliability remains a persistent challenge. Surveys report deep-learning models achieving more than 98 % accuracy on clean, controlled datasets [3, 4], whereas operational deployments frequently show performance drops of 15–30 percentage points when laboratory-trained models are applied to operational vessels [5, 6]. This degradation stems from three compounding factors: (i) operating-condition variability, including rotational-speed fluctuations, load changes along the propulsion curve and transient manoeuvres during docking; (ii) environmental degradation, including salt-spray corrosion, temperature cycling and humidity-induced sensor drift; and (iii) progressive machinery wear over multi-year service intervals, which shifts fault signatures away from laboratory-calibrated baselines.

Mechanistic simulation offers a theoretically sound pathway for supplementing scarce real-world data [7, 8]. First-principles models based on rotordynamics, thermodynamics and fluid mechanics can generate fault signatures under arbitrarily many operating conditions without physical test campaigns. However, uncalibrated simulations diverge from measured behaviour. Unmodelled structural flexure in long shaft lines, evolving bearing clearances during wear-in, lubricant-viscosity shifts caused by oil degradation and hull-borne noise from propeller cavitation collectively produce a simulator-to-measurement gap; classifiers trained directly on uncalibrated simulation data lose 15–30 percentage points of accuracy relative to classifiers trained on distribution-matched data [9, 10]. Closing this deployment gap is therefore the critical bottleneck for intelligent condition monitoring in maritime operations.

## 1.2 Problem statement and research gaps

Four shortcomings of existing cross-domain fault diagnosis methods make them unsuitable for marine power systems as they stand. No single limitation is fatal on its own, but together they create a gap that standard domain adaptation cannot bridge:

1. Mechanistic models produce synthetic data only at discrete operating points, yielding discontinuous domain bridges that fail to capture the smooth transitions observed in real ship operations. Most simulation outputs are obtained at scattered preset working points and cannot be interpolated to intermediate conditions that are ubiquitous in actual navigation.

2. Conventional domain adaptation treats operating conditions as discrete categorical domains rather than as points on a continuous manifold, and therefore cannot interpolate between predefined operating points. Classical discrete-domain algorithms assume fixed source–target pairs and degrade markedly when test samples fall between predefined categories, which is the rule rather than the exception for marine machinery under variable sea states [11].

3. Uniform sampling over the operating envelope wastes computational resources on regions of low diagnostic difficulty while under-sampling high-complexity regimes. Equal-interval grid sampling spends much of its budget on easily separated working zones, whereas boundary conditions characterised by severe aliasing of fault features receive too few samples.

4. Multi-source aggregation averages feature representations equally across all source domains without sample-wise relevance weighting, which introduces a risk of negative transfer when irrelevant source data dominate learning. When source domains with different working ranges are mixed directly, samples far from the target distribution may mislead feature learning, and negative transfer occurs frequently in marine scenarios with large distribution shift [12].

## 1.3 Contributions

This paper makes the following contributions:

1. A three-stage continuous-domain transfer framework (MS-BDTT) that integrates parameterised rotordynamic simulation, Bayesian digital-twin calibration and attention-weighted adversarial transfer into a unified pipeline. The operating envelope is modelled explicitly as a continuous manifold rather than as a discrete set of conditions, which enables smooth interpolation between source and target domains (Section 3).

2. A demand-guided sampling strategy that allocates the simulation budget non-uniformly over the operating envelope according to a composite demand map combining MMD-based domain separation and fault-sensitivity gradients. The measured budget sweep (six levels, five runs per point) shows that the rule reaches 90 % of its own full-budget accuracy with 60 samples per condition against 100 for uniform grid sampling, a measured budget-efficiency gain of 1.67×, and reaches the same 75.5 % accuracy with 60 against 80 samples (1.33×) (Section 3.3, Fig. 10, Table D.3).

3. A Bayesian particle-filter calibrator that aligns mechanistic simulations with reference sensor data using unlabelled normal-condition measurements only, and that infers the structural correction state [k, c] only — fault severity is never updated from healthy data. It contributes 21.9 percentage points to the 65.7 % cumulative simulator-to-simulator MMD reduction (RKHS with a Gaussian kernel; the continuous bridge contributes the remaining 43.8 percentage points). Identifiability of the calibration state is examined with controlled parameter-recovery, posterior-predictive-check and coverage diagnostics in Appendix E.2 (Section 3.4).

4. A sample-level attention mechanism that learns to prioritise the bridge samples most relevant to a target query and suppresses the 37 % of bridge samples with the lowest learned relevance, thereby mitigating negative transfer. The measured retention sweep (five ratios, five runs per point) places the maximum of the accuracy curve and the minimum of the domain gap at the same operating point, namely the 63 % retention ratio defined by the 37th-percentile relevance threshold (83.9 % macro-average against 81.2 % without suppression and 77.4 % at 20 % retention); the optimum is located by the sweep rather than proved (Section 3.5, Fig. 5, Table D.2).

5. A heuristic generalisation analysis for the target-domain risk, in which the source–target divergence is decomposed by subadditivity into a sum of divergences between adjacent bridge domains; the analysis is interpreted strictly as an upper-bound decomposition and an estimation-error argument, not as a demonstration that intermediate domains reduce population divergence (Section 3.7 and Appendix C).

6. A reproducibility package in which every table and figure is regenerated from one auditable source, with split manifests, per-run predictions and exact seeds deposited in a Zenodo archive (Code and Data Availability).

## 1.4 Paper organisation

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on cross-domain fault diagnosis, domain adaptation and mechanistic simulation. Section 3 presents the MS-BDTT methodology. Section 4 describes the experimental setup, including datasets with full provenance, scenario design with explicit label and partition protocols, baselines, implementation details and the evaluation protocol. Section 5 reports and discusses the results. Section 6 concludes the paper and outlines future directions. Appendices A and F provide the simulator physical kernel and the dataset provenance and split-manifest documentation; Appendices B–E provide the finite element formulation, the particle-filter pseudocode with proof details and raw MMD documentation, the hyperparameter search together with the measured-sweep protocols, and the prior-mismatch robustness and identifiability analyses.
